Tie Your Camel: Effort and Reliance on Allah at Work

Work too hard and you forget that rizq is from Allah. Work too little and you forget that effort is commanded. The Qur’an holds both.

TawakkulTie Your CamelTulona.

Two ways to get work wrong

There are two classic mistakes people make about their careers.

The first: act as if everything depends on you. Every outcome, every promotion, every deadline, every layoff. You hold your breath, you overwork, you wake at 3 a.m. replaying a meeting. You treat success as something you manufacture entirely on your own.

The second: act as if nothing depends on you. “It’s all written anyway.” “If Allah wants me to have the job, I’ll have it.” You skip the application, drift through the interview, avoid the hard task, and call it trust.

Both feel like a kind of faith. Neither is.

One short hadith holds the whole answer.

The man and his camel

A man came to the Prophet (peace be upon him) and asked: should I tie my camel and trust in Allah, or leave her untied and trust in Allah?

The Prophet said: tie her, and trust in Allah. (Jami’ at-Tirmidhi 2517)

That’s the whole teaching of tawakkul, reliance on Allah.

It isn’t laziness. It isn’t anxiety. It’s a sequence. Do the work first. Tie the knot with your own hands. Then place the result with the One who controls it.

Notice the man didn’t ask about something heroic. He asked about a camel. A daily, practical, slightly boring task. And the Prophet’s answer was just as practical.

Most of our working life is camels.

What tawakkul is not

It’s worth spelling out what this is not.

  • It’s not passivity. The Qur’an says: when you have decided, then rely upon Allah. (Qur’an 3:159) Decide first. Plan first. Act first.
  • It’s not denial. Trusting Allah doesn’t mean pretending a risk isn’t real.
  • It’s not a guarantee of the outcome you want. It’s a guarantee that the outcome you get is in the hands of the One who is wise, and merciful, and sees more than you do.

Scholars often describe tawakkul as having two parts. The hands work. The heart rests.

If either one is missing, something’s off. Hands that work with a heart that panics is exhaustion. A heart that rests with hands that don’t work is neglect.

The prophets planned

If you want to see how serious the Prophet (peace be upon him) was about preparation, look at the Hijra.

This was the most important journey in early Islamic history. If anyone could have skipped logistics and said “Allah will take care of it,” it was him.

Instead, he and Abu Bakr hired a guide. A man who knew the desert routes well, even though he was not yet a Muslim. They arranged that the camels would be handed over and the guide would meet them at a set time. (Sahih al-Bukhari 3905)

They left by night. They went south first, in the opposite direction from Madinah, to the cave of Thawr. They hid for three days. Abu Bakr’s daughter Asma brought food. His son Abdullah listened to what Quraysh were saying in Makkah and brought news after dark. A shepherd drove his flock over their tracks to erase them.

That’s a full operations plan.

And yet when pursuers got close to the cave, Abu Bakr was afraid, and the Prophet said the words the Qur’an preserves: do not grieve. Indeed, Allah is with us. (Qur’an 9:40)

Planning, and calm. Both in the same story.

The birds that leave the nest

There’s another hadith that people often quote only half of.

If you were to rely upon Allah as He should be relied upon, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds. They go out in the morning hungry and return in the evening full. (Jami’ at-Tirmidhi 2344)

Look closely. The birds go out.

They don’t sit in the nest and wait for food to arrive. They leave. They search, they fly, they work. And they come back full.

That’s the model. Not stillness, but movement with trust.

Shake the palm tree

The Qur’an has an image that teaches the same lesson.

Maryam, alone, in labour, exhausted, with nobody around her. Allah could have placed food in her hands. Instead the command was: shake toward yourself the trunk of the palm tree, and it will drop upon you ripe, fresh dates. (Qur’an 19:25)

Think about the scene. A woman in that state, being asked to shake a tree.

The miracle was real. The dates were a gift. But effort was still part of the process, even for her, even then. A small effort, within her ability, but an effort.

That’s a gentle reminder for those of us who are tired and tempted to do nothing. Do the small thing in front of you. Shake the tree. Let Allah do what only He can do.

Enter from different gates

Yaqub (peace be upon him) gave his sons advice before they travelled to Egypt: O my sons, do not enter from one gate, but enter from different gates. And I cannot avail you anything against Allah. The decision is only for Allah. Upon Him I rely, and upon Him let those who would rely, rely. (Qur’an 12:67)

He gave practical advice and in the same breath admitted the limits of that advice.

Diversify your approach. Have a plan B. Don’t put everything through one door.

And then: it’s still Allah’s decision.

That’s what working like a believer looks like. You send ten applications, not one. You build more than one skill. You keep savings if you can. You prepare thoroughly for the interview. You don’t make your fate depend on a single gate.

Then you let go of the outcome.

Strive, and don’t say “if only”

One hadith puts this all together beautifully.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: the strong believer is better and more beloved to Allah than the weak believer, though there is good in both. Strive for what benefits you, seek help from Allah, and do not give up. If something befalls you, do not say, “If only I had done such and such.” Rather say, “Allah decreed, and what He willed, He did.” For “if only” opens the door to the work of Satan. (Sahih Muslim 2664)

Break it down.

  1. Strive for what benefits you. Be deliberate. Go after what’s useful.
  2. Seek help from Allah. Make dua. Ask Him. Don’t go it alone.
  3. Don’t give up. Don’t collapse into helplessness.
  4. If it still goes wrong, don’t spiral. No endless “if only.” You did what you could.

That fourth step is where many of us struggle. After a failed project, a lost role, a missed opportunity, the mind loops: if I’d only prepared more, spoken up, applied earlier.

The hadith doesn’t say reflection is forbidden. Learning is good. But there’s a difference between “what can I learn?” and “why didn’t I stop this?” One builds you. The other eats you.

Rizq is written, and still sought

A comforting hadith says: no soul will die until it has received its full provision. So fear Allah and be moderate in seeking it. Take what is lawful and leave what is unlawful. (Reported by Ibn Majah)

Two parts again.

First, your provision has been decided. You can’t take someone else’s. Nobody can take yours.

Second, be moderate in seeking it. Not careless. Not frantic.

That second part is directly relevant to burnout. If you believe your whole financial future depends on one more weekend of overwork, you won’t be moderate. You’ll grab. You’ll compromise, cut corners, snap at people, miss prayers.

The hadith pulls you back. Seek, yes. Gently. And only through what’s lawful.

The Qur’an adds a promise: whoever relies upon Allah, then He is sufficient for them. (Qur’an 65:3)

When the plan fails

Sooner or later, you’ll do everything right and it won’t work.

The project is cancelled. The role is cut. The interview goes well and the offer goes to someone else. You were prepared, honest, hardworking, and it still doesn’t land.

This is where tawakkul gets tested.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: how wonderful is the affair of the believer. All of it is good for him, and that is for no one except the believer. If something good happens to him, he is grateful, and that is good for him. If something harmful happens to him, he is patient, and that is good for him. (Sahih Muslim 2999)

All of it is good. Even the closed door.

That doesn’t mean the disappointment won’t hurt. The Prophet himself wept. Allowing yourself to be sad is not a failure of faith.

But it does give you a lens for afterwards. The Qur’an reminds us that it may be that you dislike a thing while it is good for you, and it may be that you love a thing while it is bad for you, and Allah knows while you do not. (Qur’an 2:216)

Plenty of people look back years later and realise the rejection that crushed them was the thing that sent them somewhere better. Not everyone gets to see it. But the verse asks you to hold that possibility open.

The anxiety of checking

Here’s a very modern form of tawakkul trouble.

You send the application. You refresh your inbox. You refresh again. You check whether the message was read. You check at lunch. You check at midnight.

That’s not effort anymore. That’s the heart refusing to hand over the result.

The cure is surprisingly practical. Decide in advance when you’ll check. Once a day, say. Then close it and do something else. When the urge returns, say hasbunallah and go back to what’s in front of you.

The result will arrive at its time. Your refreshing doesn’t speed it up. It just spends your peace.

The believer’s habit is different. Do the work. Ask. Leave it. Return to life.

Rest is part of the plan

Tawakkul also gives you permission to rest.

The companion Salman al-Farisi once visited his brother Abu Darda and found him fasting every day, praying all night, neglecting his wife and himself. Salman told him: your Lord has a right over you, your own self has a right over you, and your family has a right over you. So give each its due.

When this was reported to the Prophet (peace be upon him), he said: Salman has spoken the truth. (Sahih al-Bukhari 1968)

It’s worth noticing that this correction was about worship, not even about work. Even too much devotion can be unbalanced.

So sleep. Eat properly. Take your day off. See your family. Rest isn’t laziness. It’s part of the trust. It’s you saying: I’ve done my part for today, and the rest is not in my hands.

Hasbunallah

When Ibrahim (peace be upon him) was thrown toward the fire, and when the believers were told that people had gathered a great force against them, the words they said were: Allah is sufficient for us, and He is the best Disposer of affairs. (Qur’an 3:173)

The phrase is hasbunallahu wa ni’mal wakeel.

You can say it before a hard meeting. After you’ve sent the application. When you’re waiting for results. When the call you’re dreading finally comes.

It’s not a way of avoiding effort. It’s the last line of effort. Everything you could do, you’ve done. Now you hand it over.

A short routine for any working day

  1. Plan. Decide what matters today. Write it down.
  2. Prepare. Do the groundwork before the meeting, the submission, the conversation.
  3. Act. Do the thing properly. Shake the tree.
  4. Ask. Make dua. Ask Allah to put baraka in it.
  5. Release. Say hasbunallah. Close the laptop.

Five steps. Hands working. Heart resting.

Tie your camel today. And then, truly, let her go.

Do your part properly. Then rest in His.

Which camel have you left untied?
