Love for Your Brother: Cooperation and Fairness at Work

Most workplaces run on competition. The Islamic view adds something quieter: the person beside you is not your rival.

BrotherhoodLove for Your BrotherTulona.

Competition is everywhere. Is it the whole story?

Most workplaces run on competition.

Rankings, ratings, bonuses, who gets the project, who gets noticed. Even in friendly teams there’s a quiet scoreboard, and we all know roughly where we stand on it.

Some competition is healthy. The Qur’an even tells us to race one another to good deeds. (Qur’an 2:148)

But there’s a different spirit under the Islamic view of work, one that can feel almost countercultural. It says the people beside you aren’t your rivals. They’re your brothers and sisters, and what you want for yourself, you should want for them.

None of you truly believes

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself. (Sahih al-Bukhari 13, Sahih Muslim 45)

Read it slowly. Faith, here, isn’t measured only by prayer or fasting. It’s measured by how you feel when someone else is doing well.

Take a moment to test yourself against it.

A colleague gets the promotion you hoped for. A teammate’s idea is praised in the meeting. Someone younger than you gets a better offer.

What rises first in your chest?

If it’s a flicker of envy, you’re normal. That reaction is part of being human. But the hadith gives you something to do with it. Notice it. Ask Allah to remove it. And then actively wish them well, out loud if you can.

That’s a muscle. It strengthens with use.

Like a building, like a body

The Prophet (peace be upon him) used two images to describe how believers should relate to one another.

The first: the believer to the believer is like a building, each part strengthening the other. And he interlaced his fingers to show it. (Sahih al-Bukhari 481, Sahih Muslim 2585)

Think of a brick wall. Every brick is carrying the weight of the one above it and being held by the ones around it. Remove one, and the others shift.

The second: the example of the believers in their mutual love, mercy and compassion is like one body. When one part is in pain, the whole body responds with sleeplessness and fever. (Sahih al-Bukhari 6011, Sahih Muslim 2586)

When your colleague is struggling, in a project, in their health, in their home, the idea is that you feel it too. Not as a burden, but as a sign that you belong to the same body.

That should change how we run a team. A struggling teammate isn’t a risk to be managed. They’re a part of the body that needs attention.

Help, and be helped

Another hadith spells out the practical side.

Whoever relieves a believer of a hardship in this world, Allah will relieve him of a hardship on the Day of Resurrection. Whoever eases the affairs of someone in difficulty, Allah will ease his affairs in this world and the next. And Allah helps His servant as long as the servant helps his brother. (Sahih Muslim 2699)

The last line is the part to remember. Allah helps His servant as long as the servant is helping his brother.

It turns generosity at work into a kind of insurance. Not because you’re calculating, but because the whole system runs on mercy returning to those who give it.

Think about the ordinary ways you can help.

  • Share what you know without hoarding it.
  • Offer to take a task off someone’s plate when they’re drowning.
  • Introduce a junior colleague to someone useful.
  • Cover for someone who has a family emergency.
  • Say “let me show you” instead of “I’m busy.”

None of these are dramatic. All of them are part of the same hadith.

Cooperate in goodness

The Qur’an gives the principle directly: cooperate in righteousness and piety, and do not cooperate in sin and aggression. (Qur’an 5:2)

The verse has two halves, and both matter.

First, cooperate. The default setting of a believer at work is collaboration, not silo-building.

Second, cooperate in what is good. Teamwork isn’t a blanket virtue. If the team is cutting corners, hiding errors or treating customers unfairly, loyalty to the group doesn’t justify joining in.

This is useful moral guidance. “Everyone does it” isn’t a defence. And “I was only following the group” isn’t either.

The believer is a good teammate, until the team asks them to be a bad person. Then the first loyalty is to what is right.

Consultation is a Qur’anic habit

There’s another piece that’s easy to overlook.

The Qur’an describes the believers as those whose affair is consultation among themselves. (Qur’an 42:38)

And it tells the Prophet himself: consult them in the matter. And when you have decided, then rely upon Allah. (Qur’an 3:159)

The Prophet (peace be upon him), who received revelation, still sat with his companions and asked them what they thought. Before the Battle of Badr, he listened to a companion who suggested a better position for the army, and he changed the plan.

If that was the standard of the leader of the community, what excuse do the rest of us have for not listening?

Good teams ask. Good managers ask. A leader who consults is not weaker. They’re closer to the sunna.

And again, notice the sequence. Consult. Decide. Then rely on Allah. It’s the same pattern as the camel.

When a man offered to share everything

One of the most moving stories about workplace character is almost never told that way.

When the Muhajirun migrated from Makkah to Madinah, they left behind almost everything. Houses, businesses, savings. One of them, Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf, was paired by the Prophet as a brother with Sa’d ibn al-Rabi, a wealthy man from the Ansar.

Sa’d said to him: I’m one of the wealthiest people in Madinah. Take half of my wealth. And I have two wives. Whichever one you like, I’ll divorce her so you can marry her.

Look at what’s being offered. Half of everything.

And Abdur-Rahman said: may Allah bless you in your family and your wealth. Just show me the market. (Sahih al-Bukhari 2048)

He went to the market. He started with small trades. In a short time he had built a business of his own.

Two things at once

This single story holds two lessons that seem to pull in opposite directions.

The first is generosity. Sa’d didn’t hold back. He offered half of what he had to a man he barely knew, because the Prophet had made them brothers. The Qur’an praises this spirit among the Ansar: they prefer others over themselves, even if they are in need. (Qur’an 59:9)

The second is dignity. Abdur-Rahman didn’t take it. He was grateful, he prayed for Sa’d, and then he asked for a place to work.

A good team needs both of these.

It needs people who give freely, share contacts, advice, time and opportunities. And it needs people who want to stand on their own feet and contribute, rather than wait to be carried.

Generosity without dignity can breed dependence. Dignity without generosity can breed isolation.

The Companions managed both at once.

Give credit like you mean it

One of the easiest ways to practise “love for your brother what you love for yourself” is to give credit.

When your teammate’s idea shaped the result, say so. When a junior person did the careful work behind your success, name them. When someone helped you, thank them where others can hear it.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: whoever does not thank people does not thank Allah. (Sunan Abi Dawud 4811)

Taking credit for work that isn’t yours is a small theft. Giving credit generously is a form of justice, and it costs you less than you think. People trust those who share the light.

Envy is the enemy of teamwork

If there’s one feeling that corrodes a team, it’s envy.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) warned: beware of envy, because envy consumes good deeds just as fire consumes wood. (Sunan Abi Dawud 4903)

A strong image. Envy doesn’t just make you unhappy. It quietly burns your own deeds.

The cure the tradition gives is practical.

  1. Dua for them. Ask Allah to increase them in what they have. It feels strange at first. It works.
  2. Gratitude for yourself. List what you do have. Envy thrives on blindness to your own blessings.
  3. Remember it’s not a limited pie. Their rizq didn’t come out of yours.
  4. Help them. It’s very hard to resent someone you’re actively helping.

One of the Companions was told by the Prophet that he was a man of Paradise. A curious person stayed with him for three days to see what made him special. He found no unusual night prayers or extra fasts. When asked, the man said only that he never went to bed holding a grudge or envy against any Muslim. (Musnad Ahmad)

That’s a sign of how much the heart’s cleanliness weighs.

Brothers and sisters, not just colleagues

The Qur’an says: the believers are but brothers, so make settlement between your brothers. (Qur’an 49:10)

And in the very next verse it forbids mockery, name-calling and belittling. (Qur’an 49:11)

That’s a whole teamwork philosophy in two verses. We’re family. When there’s conflict, our job is to repair, not to win. And while we’re at it, no sarcasm, nicknames or put-downs that turn a colleague into a punchline.

Of course, not everyone at work is a Muslim, and the same spirit applies more widely. The Prophet (peace be upon him) was told that Allah is kind to creation and loves kindness. Treating all colleagues fairly and respectfully is part of the character he came to perfect.

But the specific bond he described among believers adds a layer of closeness, a sense that your success is partly theirs, and theirs partly yours.

A leader’s version of this

If you manage others, all of this becomes sharper.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock. (Sahih al-Bukhari 893, Sahih Muslim 1829)

A shepherd isn’t above the flock. They’re responsible for it. They notice who is limping. They notice who’s fallen behind. They put the flock’s wellbeing before their own convenience.

For a manager, that might mean:

  • Protecting your team from blame that isn’t theirs.
  • Making sure the quiet one gets heard.
  • Paying people fairly, and on time. The Prophet said to give the worker his wages before his sweat dries. (Sunan Ibn Majah 2443)
  • Never asking someone to do something you’d refuse to do yourself.
  • Taking responsibility when things go wrong.

Leadership, in this picture, is service with accountability.

The swap test

Here’s a simple habit that puts the whole hadith to work.

Before you make a decision that affects someone else, swap seats.

If you were the colleague receiving this deadline, would it feel fair? If you were the junior person getting this feedback, would it feel respectful? If you were the person being asked to cover this shift, would you say yes?

If the answer is no, adjust.

It takes thirty seconds. And it’s one of the most direct ways to live out “love for your brother what you love for yourself.”

What a team like this feels like

Picture a team where people root for each other’s wins. Where credit is shared openly. Where nobody hides what they know. Where difficulties are raised early because there’s no shame in asking. Where feedback is gentle and honest. Where the person who’s struggling is noticed before they collapse.

Not perfect. Humans are still humans. But steady, warm and productive.

That’s not a fantasy. It’s what happens when enough individuals take a single hadith seriously.

Start with yourself. Wish for the person next to you what you wish for yourself. Then do one small thing about it today.

Want for them what you want for yourself.

What is one thing you can do for a colleague today?
