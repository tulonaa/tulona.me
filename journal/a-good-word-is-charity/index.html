A Good Word Is Charity: Speech, Kindness and the Workplace

A lot of a working day is spoken or typed. That makes the tongue one of the biggest things we manage.

SpeechA Good Word Is CharityTulona.

The part of work nobody puts in the job description

Look at any job description and you’ll find skills, tools and responsibilities.

You won’t find: speaks respectfully when stressed. Stays gentle when corrected. Doesn’t gossip about the team.

And yet that’s what people actually remember. Not the spreadsheet you built, but the way you talked to them when the deadline was tight.

Islam treats speech as serious business. The tongue is small, and the Prophet (peace be upon him) kept coming back to it, because so much of a person’s character comes out of the mouth.

A good word is charity

In a long hadith about daily charity, the Prophet (peace be upon him) listed all the ways a person can give sadaqah. Helping someone onto their mount. Lifting their luggage. Removing something harmful from the road. Walking to prayer. And among them: a good word is charity. (Sahih al-Bukhari 2989, Sahih Muslim 1009)

A good word. Charity.

No money leaves your wallet. No one needs a receipt. And yet it counts on the same page as the coins you give.

At work, the opportunities come all day.

  • Thanking someone properly, not with a rushed “thanks.”
  • Telling a colleague their presentation helped you understand the problem.
  • Greeting the cleaner by name.
  • Saying “take your time” to someone who’s clearly struggling.
  • Answering a basic question without making it sound basic.

Each one is a small act of charity, and each one costs nothing but the decision to do it.

Smile, it’s sadaqah

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: your smiling in the face of your brother is charity. (Jami’ at-Tirmidhi 1956)

It’s almost comically simple.

Anyone who has worked in an office knows how much a first impression of a face matters. The colleague who looks up and smiles when you walk past. The manager who doesn’t scowl when you knock. The person on the phone whose voice is audibly warm.

These tiny signals set the temperature of a team. Fear spreads quickly. So does ease.

And remember, it’s sadaqah. Which means that on a long Monday, when you really don’t feel like smiling, you can still do it as an act of worship.

Say what is best

The Qur’an gives a very direct instruction: tell My servants to say that which is best. Indeed, Satan induces dissension among them. (Qur’an 17:53)

That’s a warning about the way ordinary conversation can slide. A sharp remark, a joke at someone’s expense, a comment that’s technically true but meant to wound.

It says Satan stirs up trouble between people, and the way he does it is often through words.

You’ve probably seen it. A small misunderstanding in a group chat. A tone that’s misread. A reply that’s a little too quick. Within a day, two reasonable people are barely speaking.

The instruction isn’t only “don’t be rude.” It’s “reach for the best version of what you could say.” Not just acceptable. Best.

Elsewhere the Qur’an says: speak good to people. (Qur’an 2:83)

Speak good or stay silent

One of the most quoted hadith on speech goes: whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent. (Sahih al-Bukhari 6018, Sahih Muslim 47)

Two options. Speak good, or stay silent.

There is no third option called “speak badly, but accurately.”

That rule is a gift for working life, because so much of work speech sits in the grey area. The vent that’s technically honest but really just bitter. The comment you’d never say to someone’s face. The email you write when your pulse is up.

The hadith gives you a filter. Before you speak, ask: is this good? If not, is staying silent an option?

Often it is. And almost always, silence is cheaper than repair.

The email you don’t send

Let’s make it concrete.

It’s 9 p.m. Someone has said something that stung. You write a reply. It’s sharp, clever and completely justified. Your thumb hovers over send.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: the strong person is not the one who overcomes others in wrestling. The strong person is the one who controls himself at the time of anger. (Sahih al-Bukhari 6114, Sahih Muslim 2609)

And when a man asked him for advice, he repeated several times: do not become angry. (Sahih al-Bukhari 6116)

The strongest move at 9 p.m. is often to save the draft. Walk away. Make wudu. That’s not only a spiritual habit. Cold water on the face helps a heated nervous system.

In the morning, read it again.

Most of the time you’ll delete half of it. Sometimes you’ll delete all of it and have a quiet conversation instead. Occasionally you’ll send it with two changes, and it’ll be a much better email.

Repel evil with what is better

There’s a verse in Surah Fussilat that deserves to be on every workplace wall.

The good deed and the evil deed are not equal. Repel evil with what is better, and then the one between whom and you was enmity will become as though he were a close friend. (Qur’an 41:34)

Imagine applying that to the colleague you can’t stand.

The one who takes credit. The one who interrupts. The one who gives unfair feedback.

The natural reaction is to respond in kind, or to withdraw coldly. The verse suggests a third option: answer with something better. A polite correction instead of a sarcastic one. A genuine acknowledgment when they do something well. A calm tone when theirs isn’t.

It won’t always work. The next verse says this quality is only granted to those who are patient, and to those who have a great share of good character. (Qur’an 41:35)

It’s not easy. It’s a skill. But the promise in the verse is real: the person who was a rival can become a friend.

Gentleness is not weakness

Some of us worry that kindness at work reads as soft. That to be taken seriously, you need an edge.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said otherwise. Allah is gentle and loves gentleness, and He gives for gentleness what He does not give for harshness. (Sahih Muslim 2593)

And: gentleness is not found in anything except that it beautifies it, and it is not removed from anything except that it disfigures it. (Sahih Muslim 2594)

Beautifies. Disfigures.

The same piece of feedback, delivered gently or harshly, lands in completely different ways. The first is heard. The second is defended against.

The Qur’an says this about the Prophet himself: it was by the mercy of Allah that you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have dispersed from around you. (Qur’an 3:159)

Even the Prophet, with revelation behind him, was told that harshness would have scattered people.

If a leader of that stature needed gentleness to hold a community together, none of us gets to skip it.

How the Prophet spoke to the people who worked for him

If you want to see what good speech looks like when someone has power over you, look at Anas ibn Malik.

Anas served the Prophet (peace be upon him) as a boy for about ten years. He was, in modern terms, a junior assistant. He made mistakes. Children do.

And this is what he later reported: I served the Prophet for ten years, and he never said “uff” to me. He never said to me about something I’d done, “why did you do that?” and never about something I hadn’t done, “why didn’t you do it?” (Sahih al-Bukhari 6038, Sahih Muslim 2309)

Not once. Not even an exasperated sigh.

Think about the managers you’ve met, and think about the one you’d want to be. How often does a junior colleague hear a sigh, a tight “I asked you to do that yesterday,” a sharp “why would you do it that way?”

It’s remarkable that the leader of an entire community spoke to a young helper like that.

The standard it sets is high. But it’s a clear picture of what authority looks like when it’s anchored in good character. The most powerful person in the room is also the one who is most careful with their words.

Gossip is the quiet workplace disease

Few offices are free of it.

A bit of commentary about the manager. A small joke at the expense of the new person. A “can you believe what she said?” in the corridor.

The Qur’an addresses this directly: do not spy on each other, and do not backbite. Would any of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother? You would detest it. (Qur’an 49:12)

It’s one of the most vivid images in the Qur’an, deliberately so. It’s meant to turn the stomach.

The hadith defines backbiting as mentioning your brother in a way he would dislike. When a companion asked, “what if what I say is true?”, the Prophet said: if it’s true, you have backbitten him. If it’s not true, you have slandered him. (Sahih Muslim 2589)

That closes the loophole. “But it’s true” is not a defence.

There are narrow exceptions, such as reporting genuine wrongdoing to someone who can fix it, or seeking advice about a real problem. But venting for the sake of venting isn’t one of them.

A practical rule: if you wouldn’t say it with the person standing beside you, don’t say it behind them.

Feedback with care

None of this means never saying hard things.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: the religion is sincere advice, nasihah. (Sahih Muslim 55) Wanting what’s best for someone includes telling them when they’re making a mistake.

The art is in the how.

  • Do it privately. Praise in public, correct in private.
  • Be specific. “This section confuses me because the numbers don’t match” is more useful than “this isn’t good.”
  • Start from care. Make it clear you’re saying it because you want them to succeed.
  • Offer help. Pointing out a problem is easier than helping to fix it. Do both.
  • Be ready to hear it back. If you give feedback, receive it too.

Sincere advice, delivered gently, is one of the most valuable things a colleague can offer. It’s charity of a very real kind.

Gratitude is part of speech

There’s another small hadith with large consequences. Whoever does not thank people has not thanked Allah. (Sunan Abi Dawud 4811, Jami’ at-Tirmidhi 1954)

Which means that thanking the person who helped you is not just good manners. It’s linked to your thankfulness to Allah.

Say it clearly. Say it specifically. “Thank you for staying late on that” is better than “thanks.” “Thank you for explaining it so patiently” is better still.

And say it up as well as down. Managers need to be thanked too. So do the people who clean the kitchen.

A week of better words

If you’d like to try this, here’s a simple challenge.

  1. Monday: Greet everyone you meet with a genuine smile.
  2. Tuesday: Thank three people specifically.
  3. Wednesday: Don’t say a single critical thing about someone who isn’t present.
  4. Thursday: Wait ten minutes before sending any message written in frustration.
  5. Friday: Give one piece of kind, honest feedback to someone.

Then look at the week.

Chances are you’ll have given more charity in five days of speech than in a month of small change.

And it won’t have cost you a single coin.

Speak good, or stay silent.

Whose day could your words make easier today?
