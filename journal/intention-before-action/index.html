Intention Before Action: What Niyyah Teaches Me About Work

If work takes most of our best hours, it deserves more than autopilot. It starts with one question: why am I doing this?

IntentionIntention Before ActionTulona.

Work is where most of us spend our best hours

Think about your week.

How many hours go to sleep? To family? To worship? To work?

For most of us, work wins or comes close. We give it our sharpest mornings, our longest concentration, our most patient energy.

So here is a question worth sitting with. If work takes this much of a life, where does it sit in the life of a Muslim?

Is it a necessary distraction from worship? A tax we pay to afford the rest of life? Or is it something more?

The answer starts with the very first hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari.

Actions are judged by intentions

Umar ibn al-Khattab reported that the Prophet (peace be upon him) said: actions are judged by intentions, and every person will get what they intended. (Sahih al-Bukhari 1, Sahih Muslim 1907)

Imam al-Bukhari opened his collection with it. Many scholars have called it a third of knowledge. That tells you how much weight it carries.

Notice what the hadith does not say. It does not say “religious actions are judged by intentions.” It says actions. All of them.

Waking up early. Writing a report. Answering a customer. Cleaning an office. Teaching a class. Running a shop.

Two people can do the exact same task and receive completely different outcomes with Allah, because of what was in their hearts when they began.

One works only to be admired. The other works to provide for a family, to be useful to people, and to earn in a way that is halal. Same desk. Same hours. Different book of deeds.

Qur’an 98:5Sincere to Him in everything.Tulona.

The one thing every deed needs

The Qur’an says people were commanded only to worship Allah, sincere to Him in religion. (Qur’an 98:5)

Sincerity, ikhlas, is the word scholars keep returning to. It means the deed is for Allah first, and everything else comes second.

This does not mean you must never enjoy recognition, or never want a promotion. Wanting to do well is human. The Prophet (peace be upon him) himself said that a person who earns to provide for their family is rewarded for it.

The problem starts when approval becomes the only fuel. When the applause is the reason, and the work collapses the moment the applause stops.

Sincerity is quieter. It keeps going when no one is watching. It does the careful version of the job even when a careless version would pass.

Every job can be worship

Here is the part many of us miss. The Qur’an teaches the believer to say: my prayer, my sacrifice, my living and my dying are for Allah, Lord of all the worlds. (Qur’an 6:162)

My living. Not only my praying.

The ordinary parts of a day, the commute, the meetings, the emails, the spreadsheet, can all be lifted by the right intention. Earning a halal income so you don’t have to depend on others. Supporting the people who depend on you. Doing a job that actually helps someone.

Nothing about the task itself changes. The meaning of it does.

And the Qur’an places work right beside worship. After the Friday prayer, it says: when the prayer is finished, disperse through the land and seek the bounty of Allah, and remember Allah often so you may succeed. (Qur’an 62:10)

Pray. Then go to work. Remember Allah while you do.

The verse doesn’t treat the two as rivals. It treats them as a rhythm.

Allah looks at hearts and deeds

There’s another hadith worth keeping close. Allah does not look at your appearance or your wealth. He looks at your hearts and your deeds. (Sahih Muslim 2564)

Think what that does to the way we measure a career.

Job titles do not show up in that sentence. Neither do salaries, followers or the size of the office. What shows up is the heart behind the work, and what the work actually did.

A cleaner who does their job with sincerity and care may be far ahead of a director who is distracted, rushed and proud. We can’t see it from the outside. Allah can.

Sahih Muslim 2564He looks at hearts and deeds.Tulona.

The check before you begin

Scholars of the heart used to recommend something simple. Renew your intention before you begin.

Not once a year. Not on a poster. Before the task.

You can do it in ten seconds, while the laptop loads or the kettle boils. Ask yourself three questions.

  1. Why am I doing this? Be honest. Fear, ego and money are allowed on the list, but they shouldn’t be the only things on it.
  2. Who benefits? Name them. A colleague, a client, a family, a community.
  3. Is this halal, and am I doing it properly? Not just permitted, but done with care.

Then say bismillah and begin.

It’s a small habit. But small habits are how a heart changes direction.

Doing it well is part of the intention

A sincere intention without effort is only half the story.

There is a report, graded hasan by some scholars, that Allah loves that when one of you does a job, he does it with excellence. (Reported by al-Bayhaqi)

The Arabic idea behind this is itqan: doing something with mastery, with finish, with care for the details nobody will ever check.

And above it sits ihsan, which the Prophet (peace be upon him) explained in the famous hadith of Jibril: to worship Allah as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, then know that He sees you. (Sahih Muslim 8)

Take that sentence to work with you.

If you believed Allah was watching your next task, would you rush it? Would you cut the corner? Would you leave the small mistake in because it’s probably fine?

Ihsan isn’t perfectionism. It’s awareness. It makes the unseen parts of your work worth doing right.

Sahih Muslim 8As though you see Him.Tulona.

When the intention drifts

It will drift. That’s normal.

You start a role with real purpose. Two years later you’re counting the minutes until Friday. Or you start doing something for Allah and slowly notice that you’re doing it for the praise.

Don’t panic. And don’t decide the whole thing was fake.

The scholars tell us to treat intention as something we repair, again and again. Stop. Ask for forgiveness. Reset. Continue.

A few practical ways to do that:

  • Make a short dua before you start your day. Ask Allah to make your work sincere and beneficial.
  • Keep one private good deed that nobody knows about.
  • Notice when you want to be seen, and ask why.
  • Give some of what you earn in charity, quietly. It loosens the grip money can have on the heart.

None of these are dramatic. All of them protect the heart from slowly shrinking to the size of a paycheck.

What changes when work is worship

Something shifts, usually quietly.

A bad day hurts less, because the day was never only about results. A good day humbles you, because you know the result wasn’t entirely yours. Criticism becomes feedback you can use. Praise becomes something you hand back to Allah.

You stop treating colleagues as obstacles or competitors, because the real audience is somewhere else.

And rest becomes easier. When your sense of worth is tied to Allah’s acceptance rather than the day’s output, you can close the laptop without guilt.

None of this makes the job easier. It makes you steadier inside it.

What if I don’t love my job?

This is the honest question underneath all of this.

Plenty of us work jobs we didn’t dream about. Some of us are in roles we took because bills were due. Some of us are in the right field but a tiring team.

Does intention rescue a job you don’t love?

Not magically. Intention doesn’t change your boredom. It does give the boredom a meaning.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) taught that earning a lawful living is itself a form of dignity. He said that no one has ever eaten better food than that which he earns from the work of his own hands, and that the Prophet of Allah, Dawud (peace be upon him), used to eat from the work of his hands. (Sahih al-Bukhari 2072)

A prophet, a king, who still worked with his hands.

He also said it is better for one of you to take a rope, gather firewood, carry it on his back and sell it, than to go around asking people who may or may not give. (Sahih al-Bukhari 1470)

Read those two together. Work is never shameful. Needing no one because you earned honestly is a quiet kind of honour.

So on the day you don’t love the job, you can still love what it lets you do. Feed a family. Give charity. Stay independent. Be of use.

That’s not a consolation prize. It’s a legitimate reason.

Intention in the small moments

Big intentions are easy to state. The test is the small moments.

The email you really don’t want to answer. The colleague who is difficult. The task nobody else will notice. The deadline that tempts you to cut a corner.

This is where the hadith earns its place.

When a nurse changes a bed at three in the morning, when a driver is patient in traffic with a passenger who is rude, when an accountant double-checks a figure nobody will audit, when a teacher stays five minutes to explain a concept to the child who is behind, each of them can turn the moment into worship by the intention they carry into it.

The task stays ordinary. The weight of it does not.

That’s what the scholars meant when they said a good intention can turn habits into worship.

A warning about showing off

It would be dishonest to talk about sincerity and skip its opposite.

The Prophet (peace be upon him) warned about riya, doing good to be seen. He called it the minor shirk, and said it was what he feared most for his community. (Musnad Ahmad)

That’s a sobering warning, and the workplace is full of temptations to it. A career is built on visibility. Updates, presentations, the polished profile, the quiet pride in being noticed.

None of that is forbidden. But it’s worth asking regularly: would I still do this if nobody ever knew?

If the answer is no, pause. Not to punish yourself, but to recalibrate.

Scholars of the heart have often compared the heart to a mirror. Showing off breathes on it and clouds it. Sincerity wipes it clear. The job of the believer is to keep wiping.

A small practice for this week

Try this for seven days.

Before the first task of the day, pause for a moment. Say bismillah. Make one clear intention out loud or in your head: I’m doing this to be useful, to earn halal, and to do it properly for Allah’s sake.

That’s it. No notebook. No app. Just one deliberate breath before the work begins.

At the end of the week, ask yourself whether the days felt different.

My guess is that they will.

Fix the intention. Then do the work with excellence.

What is your day really for?
